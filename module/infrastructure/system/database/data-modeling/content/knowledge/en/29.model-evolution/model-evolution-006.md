# Primary Key Migration, Entity Splitting, and Entity Merging

## Learning Objectives

After this lesson, you will be able to:

- Distinguish **business identifiers**, **physical primary keys**, **surrogate keys**, **public identifiers**, and **foreign keys** as a data model evolves.
- Plan key migrations through **expand – backfill – transitional sync – validate – cutover – contract** phases without losing relationships or shifting ID semantics.
- Split an entity into multiple new entities by **grain, cardinality, ownership, lifecycle, and invariants**, rather than merely moving columns to another table.
- Merge semantically identical entities via **identity-resolution rules, canonical IDs, alias tables, and conflict-resolution policies**, while preserving historical events and records.
- Use PostgreSQL queries to add keys, repoint foreign keys, detect missing/wrong references, and reconcile business measures.
- Design compatibility across APIs, events, caches, read models, and analytics during transitions, even with multiple application versions running concurrently.
- Assess rollback viability, late-arriving data, recycled keys, multi-tenant authorization, security impacts, and operational cost before dropping legacy structures.

This lesson belongs to the **Model Evolution** chapter of **DATABASE_DATA_MODELING**. The module turns business requirements into models with clear entities, relationships, constraints, keys, indexes, and physical schemas—comparing relational, document, graph, and analytical designs to keep structures evolvable and verifiable. Here, changing keys, splitting, or merging entities is treated as a **controlled semantic and data-contract change**, never merely an `ALTER TABLE` command.

> Guiding principle: before altering structure, prove **what one row, one ID, and one relationship currently mean**; after the change, prove that meaning is preserved or changed per an approved business decision.

## 1. Three Changes That Look Similar Yet Carry Different Impacts

Suppose a sales system initially has customer and order tables. Later, three requests arrive:

1. Customer key **`customer_id TEXT`** was generated by a legacy system and risks changing or colliding on import; the team wants **`customer_sk BIGINT`** for database relationships.
2. Table **`orders`** holds both commercial facts and recipient details; the team wants to split out delivery data to narrow PII exposure and give it an independent lifecycle.
3. After importing from two systems, customers **`C-101`** and **`C-202`** are verified as the same business account; the team wants to merge them so the customer is not double-counted.

Although all three may touch multiple tables, their natures differ:

| Change | Core Question | What Must Be Preserved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary key migration** | How is an existing entity identified by a new key? | Uniqueness, all FKs, public IDs, history |
| **Entity split** | Which attributes truly share the same grain and lifecycle? | Cardinality, invariants, transactions, permissions |
| **Entity merge** | Do two records represent the same business entity? | Identity rules, relationships, documents, permissions, history |

**Changing a key does not necessarily change the entity.** **Splitting an entity does not necessarily change public IDs.** **Merging records does not necessarily mean rewriting every historical key.**

Naming the change type prevents turning index optimization into a customer-identity overhaul, or trying to solve customer identity verification with an `UPDATE` FK script.

## 2. Preparation: Define Identity, Grain, and Scope Before Migration

### 2.1. One Entity Can Carry Multiple Types of Keys

| Key Type | Example | Owner | Expose Externally? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Natural/business key** | Customer code in legacy system | Business domain or integration source | Only if contract permits |
| **Surrogate key** | `customer_sk = 912348` | Owning database or service | Usually unnecessary |
| **Public identifier** | UUID `customerRef` | API/integration contract | Yes, still requires authorization checks |
| **Composite scope key** | `(tenant_id, customer_id)` | Tenant partitioning contract | Useful internally to enforce tenant scope |
| **Foreign key** | `orders.customer_sk` | Child table referencing the target ID version | Usually an implementation detail |
| **Idempotency key** | `client_request_id` on order creation | Business operation | Not an entity identifier |
| **Historical reference** | `customer_id_at_purchase` on documents | Historical transaction source | Amend only per document policy |

A **UUID does not automatically grant security**, and a **`BIGINT` is not automatically bad**. What matters is stability, uniqueness scope, reissue rules, ownership, and ability to evolve without breaking external contracts.

### 2.2. Ask Questions by Grain

Before altering **`customers`**, define:

> One row of `customers` represents **one customer account within a tenant**, not necessarily one unique natural person.

Before altering **`orders`**, define:

> One row of `orders` represents **one order assigned an `order_id`** within a tenant; multiple orders may share a customer, and each order may have multiple line items.

For a delivery snapshot:

> One row of `order_delivery_snapshots` represents **the locked address/recipient for one shipment or one order**, depending on the chosen business model.

If two accounts are labeled "same customer" while one is corporate and one is personal—or across two tenants unauthorized to merge—merging breaks authorization. **Grain and identity scope must be verified before selecting keys.**

### 2.3. Minimal Dependency Map

~~~text
customers (customer_id)
      |
      +---- orders.customer_id
      |         |
      |         +---- order_lines
      |         +---- payments / refunds
      |         +---- shipments
      |
      +---- loyalty_accounts
      +---- support_tickets
      +---- API / events / caches / search
      +---- dimension_customer / analytics facts
~~~

Never inspect only FKs declared inside PostgreSQL. Look for **external references**: IDs inside messages, URLs, partner CSV exports, cache keys, analytics datasets, CDC streams, dashboards, and ops tools. If a system stores IDs as JSON strings, the database cannot see them as FKs.

## 3. Safe Migration Framework: Expand – Migrate – Contract

A key or structural change can be split into phases:

| Phase | Goal | Exit Criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0. Discovery & contract lock** | Understand identity, dependencies, invariants, workload | Owners, source map, and recovery plan ready |
| **1. Expand** | Add new columns/tables/keys/indexes without dropping old ones | Legacy applications continue running |
| **2. Backfill** | Populate existing data into the new structure | Checkpointed, reconciled, no backward overwrites |
| **3. Transitional sync** | All new writes preserve both old and new read paths | Zero write gap; retries and errors handled |
| **4. Validate** | Verify data and all references | Zero bad nulls, orphans, mismatches, or auth leaks |
| **5. Cutover** | Shift readers/writers to the new contract | Telemetry, rollback, or remediation ready |
| **6. Contract** | Drop old structures once zero dependencies remain | No consumers or retention rules require them |

In continuously available systems, **this does not require halting all writes**. Still, "zero downtime" is viable only when apps, databases, and pipelines stay compatible across transition, with sufficient locking/sync design and resource headroom. Massive migrations may still warrant an announced maintenance window.

In particular, **code rollback** can be straightforward while **data rollback** after dropping old keys or ambiguously merging two people may be impossible. Design recovery before taking irreversible steps.

## 4. Example 1 — Migrating Customer Key From `TEXT` to Surrogate `BIGINT`

Suppose PostgreSQL currently has:

~~~sql
CREATE TABLE customers (
    tenant_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
    customer_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    display_name TEXT NOT NULL,
    created_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
    CONSTRAINT customers_pkey
        PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, customer_id)
);

CREATE TABLE orders (
    tenant_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
    order_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    customer_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    confirmed_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
    confirmed_amount NUMERIC(18, 2) NOT NULL,
    currency CHAR(3) NOT NULL,
    CONSTRAINT orders_pkey
        PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, order_id),
    CONSTRAINT fk_orders_customer_old
        FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, customer_id)
        REFERENCES customers (tenant_id, customer_id)
);

CREATE INDEX ix_orders_customer_old
    ON orders (tenant_id, customer_id, confirmed_at DESC);
~~~

**Technical goal:** add **`customer_sk BIGINT`** and eventually have **`orders`** reference **`(tenant_id, customer_sk)`**, while **`customer_id`** remains as a business code during compatibility.

**Do not alter `order_id` semantics**, do not mint new `customer_id`s, do not change confirmed prices, and do not move orders across tenants.

### 4.1. Expand Phase on Parent Table

~~~sql
CREATE SEQUENCE customer_sk_seq AS BIGINT;

ALTER TABLE customers
ADD COLUMN customer_sk BIGINT;

ALTER TABLE customers
ALTER COLUMN customer_sk
SET DEFAULT nextval('customer_sk_seq'::regclass);

ALTER SEQUENCE customer_sk_seq
OWNED BY customers.customer_sk;
~~~

This **does not alter the primary key yet**. New inserts using the default receive `customer_sk`; existing rows start `NULL` and must be populated. The sequence generates technical IDs; if applications can supply explicit values, enforce duplicate controls. In multi-tenant setups, surrogates here come from a shared sequence while the **composite FK with `tenant_id`** guards tenant boundaries.

On large tables, avoid a huge `UPDATE` holding long transactions, flooding WAL, and spiking load. Populate in checkpointed batches:

~~~sql
-- Run repeatedly in a bounded, retriable job.
WITH batch AS (
    SELECT ctid
    FROM customers
    WHERE customer_sk IS NULL
    LIMIT 1000
    FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED
)
UPDATE customers AS c
SET customer_sk = nextval('customer_sk_seq'::regclass)
FROM batch AS b
WHERE c.ctid = b.ctid
RETURNING c.tenant_id, c.customer_id, c.customer_sk;
~~~

**`ctid`** is used **only here** to grab an arbitrary current batch of rows in PostgreSQL; it is not a business key, must never be stored across runs as a durable ID, and changes when rows update. Real jobs should checkpoint on a stable key, monitor remaining `NULL` counts, and restart safely. **`SKIP LOCKED`** prevents workers from blocking on the same rows, but does not by itself prove every row has been processed.

After backfilling and ensuring new writers always supply `customer_sk`, add a unique index:

~~~sql
-- Run explicitly outside a transaction block.
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX CONCURRENTLY ux_customers_tenant_sk
    ON customers (tenant_id, customer_sk);
~~~

**`CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` cannot run inside a transaction block** and may take a long time; watch locks, invalid-index failures, storage, and load impact. A unique index on a nullable column permits multiple `NULL`s under standard PostgreSQL `NULL` handling, so it **does not yet prove completeness**.

### 4.2. Expand Phase on Child Table

~~~sql
ALTER TABLE orders
ADD COLUMN customer_sk BIGINT;

-- New index for post-cutover JOINs and lookups.
CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY ix_orders_tenant_customer_sk
    ON orders (tenant_id, customer_sk, confirmed_at DESC);
~~~

Next, update new writers: when creating an order, look up **`customer_sk`** from `customers` for **`(tenant_id, customer_id)`**, and write **both `customer_id` and `customer_sk`** from **the same verified mapping**.

Never let two input paths supply disagreeing values for the same customer. Writing both columns in one transaction is still unsafe if the inputs used to derive them differ or one side is updated by a legacy event. A DB trigger/bridge can derive the new key from the old key during transition, provided write permissions, missing-customer errors, and trigger query overhead are handled—never treat both columns as independent masters.

### 4.3. Backfill New Foreign Key on `orders`

~~~sql
-- In real jobs, bound each pass by tenant/order_id.
UPDATE orders AS o
SET customer_sk = c.customer_sk
FROM customers AS c
WHERE o.tenant_id = c.tenant_id
  AND o.customer_id = c.customer_id
  AND o.customer_sk IS NULL
  AND c.customer_sk IS NOT NULL;
~~~

This is a **transformation template**, not advice to run an unbounded `UPDATE` on a billion-row table. Batch by **`(tenant_id, order_id)`** with checkpoints; concurrent writes may continue. After backfill, catch up late-written rows and re-verify before cutover.

**Update only when `customer_sk IS NULL`**: if a row already has a `customer_sk` that disagrees with `customer_id`, never silently overwrite it without investigation. Mismatches can reveal tenant bugs, dual-write flaws, stale events, or unannounced identity changes.

### 4.4. Add FK and Full Constraints Once Write Paths Are Ready

Since **`ux_customers_tenant_sk`** is a non-partial unique index, it can serve as an FK target:

~~~sql
ALTER TABLE orders
ADD CONSTRAINT fk_orders_customer_new
FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, customer_sk)
REFERENCES customers (tenant_id, customer_sk)
NOT VALID;
~~~

**`NOT VALID`** defers scanning all historical rows when adding a PostgreSQL foreign key, while **enforcing the constraint on new writes** immediately. Because the new key is still nullable, the FK does not reject rows with `customer_sk NULL`. Add `NOT NULL` in a separate step **after** writers have switched and backfill is complete.

~~~sql
ALTER TABLE orders
VALIDATE CONSTRAINT fk_orders_customer_new;

-- Add only once no writer omits customer_sk.
ALTER TABLE orders
ADD CONSTRAINT ck_orders_customer_sk_present
CHECK (customer_sk IS NOT NULL)
NOT VALID;

ALTER TABLE orders
VALIDATE CONSTRAINT ck_orders_customer_sk_present;

ALTER TABLE orders
ALTER COLUMN customer_sk SET NOT NULL;
~~~

**`VALIDATE CONSTRAINT`** still incurs some I/O and locking; **`SET NOT NULL`** scan/lock behavior depends on PostgreSQL version and how validated constraints are leveraged. Test on production-like staging data, pick the right window, and monitor locks.

Similarly, before promoting `customer_sk` into `customers`' primary key, verify **`customers.customer_sk IS NOT NULL`** via checks or validated constraints. Never mistake a unique index on a nullable column for proof that all rows have a new ID.

### 4.5. Reconciling Both Identifiers

A query detecting orders missing mappings, pointing to the wrong customer, or crossing tenants:

~~~sql
SELECT
    o.tenant_id,
    o.order_id,
    o.customer_id AS old_customer_id,
    o.customer_sk AS new_customer_sk,
    c_old.customer_sk AS expected_customer_sk,
    c_new.customer_id AS referenced_customer_id,
    CASE
        WHEN c_old.customer_id IS NULL
            THEN 'OLD_REFERENCE_MISSING'
        WHEN o.customer_sk IS NULL
            THEN 'NEW_REFERENCE_MISSING'
        WHEN c_new.customer_sk IS NULL
            THEN 'NEW_REFERENCE_INVALID'
        WHEN o.customer_sk IS DISTINCT FROM c_old.customer_sk
            THEN 'IDENTITY_MISMATCH'
        ELSE 'MATCH'
    END AS mapping_state
FROM orders AS o
LEFT JOIN customers AS c_old
    ON c_old.tenant_id = o.tenant_id
   AND c_old.customer_id = o.customer_id
LEFT JOIN customers AS c_new
    ON c_new.tenant_id = o.tenant_id
   AND c_new.customer_sk = o.customer_sk;
~~~

Evaluate on a **consistent snapshot** under concurrent updates; separate transient lag in an async pipeline from permanent corruption. In the demo schema with the legacy FK active, missing old customers should already be blocked, yet this query remains valuable on staging, cross-store migrations, or engines without enforced FKs.

Reconciliation must check more than "zero `NULL`s":

- **`COUNT` of customers and orders** before/after, sliced by tenant and status.
- **`COUNT(DISTINCT (tenant_id, customer_id))`** in source vs. distinct mapped keys.
- **No two `customer_sk`s** for the same customer in a published state.
- **Zero orders moved across tenants**, zero lost or multiplied orders when switching JOINs.
- **Total `confirmed_amount` per currency** unchanged by key migration.
- **Customer A's API never returns Customer B's orders** under the new lookup path.
- Consumers relying on old IDs continue working or migrate per contract.

### 4.6. Swapping the Actual `PRIMARY KEY`: Not Always Needed

Once `customers` has **`customer_sk UNIQUE NOT NULL`** and `orders` enforces the new FK, the system may **meet all technical goals without immediately swapping the `PRIMARY KEY` constraint**. Legacy paths can keep the old key while new read/write relationships use the surrogate key.

If you truly intend to swap **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, customer_id)`** to **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, customer_sk)`**, verify:

1. Every FK referencing the **old PK constraint**, including tables outside the core diagram.
2. All queries, ORM mappings, triggers, import jobs, replication, CDC, and consumers relying on the old PK.
3. **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, customer_id)`** remains enforced if the business key must stay unique.
4. Every `customers` row has `customer_sk`, and child tables have migrated their FKs.
5. Lock plans, DDL duration, and rollback steps if the constraint swap fails.

**`DROP CONSTRAINT customers_pkey`** will **fail if FKs still depend on it**; never blindly add **`CASCADE`** to force-drop protective constraints. Swap constraints and indexes via a migration plan rehearsed on representative data.

**Lesson:** swapping the primary key in the DB catalog is merely the final step. **What matters is consistent identity and relationships across every access path.**

## 5. Handling Harder Key-Migration Cases

### 5.1. Mutated or Recycled Natural Keys

If a source changes **`customer_id = C-101`** to **`C-202`**, distinguish:

- **Same customer renamed code**: keep the entity and surrogate ID, add an alias for the old code as long as needed.
- **Two distinct customers collided on a code**: resolve the conflict; do not merge data.
- **Old code reissued to another person**: track namespaces, validity windows, or versioned source IDs; otherwise joining past orders by code links the wrong person.

An alias table may need **`source_system`**, **`tenant_id`**, **`external_id`**, **`effective_from`**, and **`effective_to`** when sources recycle IDs. Never enforce a bare `UNIQUE(external_id)` if IDs are only unique per system or time period.

### 5.2. Public IDs Should Not Change Just Because Physical Keys Change

If an API promised a stable **`publicCustomerId`**, do not start returning the new `customer_sk` in its place. Clients bookmark, embed, email, export, and store public IDs externally.

You can maintain:

~~~text
customer_sk             -- new technical key
customer_id             -- legacy traceable code
public_customer_id      -- stable API/integration ID
~~~

Each key has its own contract and lifecycle. If a public ID must truly change, provide **mapping/redirect tables, compatibility policies, consumer tracking, and alias retirement dates**.

### 5.3. Multi-Tenancy and Relationship Scope

An FK on **`customer_sk`** alone may technically identify a row if surrogates are globally unique, yet you must still enforce **tenant authorization**. A composite FK **`(tenant_id, customer_sk)`** as shown earlier also prevents an order in tenant A from referencing a customer in tenant B when mapped properly.

Even with a valid FK, **knowing an ID never grants permission to read** another customer's data. Enforce tenant/principal checks in the API or trusted DB policies; never rely on ID unguessability as your sole security control.

## 6. Example 2 — Entity Split: From Orders to Orders + Delivery Details

A completely different problem from key migration: **`orders`** currently mixes **commercial facts** with **snapshotted recipient/address details**:

~~~text
orders
  tenant_id, order_id, customer_id
  confirmed_at, confirmed_amount, currency, status
  recipient_name, recipient_phone, address_line1, city
~~~

Sales reporting needs only commercial fields. Fulfillment needs recipient details **for shipped orders**. Keeping everything in one table widens PII exposure whenever any service reads orders.

### 6.1. Pick the Right Grain for the New Entity

There are at least three distinct options:

| Model | Grain | When Appropriate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`order_delivery_snapshots`** | One address snapshot per **order** | Every order has one locked address and never splits parcels |
| **`order_shipments`** | One parcel/shipment per **order** | One order can split into multiple parcels with independent delivery states |
| **`shipment_delivery_revisions`** | One delivery edit per **shipment** | Post-confirmation address edits are allowed and require audit history |

If an order can split into two parcels going to two addresses, PK **`(tenant_id, order_id)`** for all delivery data is **too coarse**. If the business always has one address per order, multi-level revision tables add needless complexity.

In the following example, assume **one physical order has one delivery snapshot at confirmation**; later address edits are a separate audited workflow.

### 6.2. Expand: Add an Independent Table While Preserving Relations

Given `orders` keyed by **`(tenant_id, order_id)`**, create:

~~~sql
CREATE TABLE order_delivery_snapshots (
    tenant_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
    order_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    recipient_name TEXT NOT NULL,
    recipient_phone TEXT,
    address_line1 TEXT NOT NULL,
    city TEXT NOT NULL,
    captured_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
    PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, order_id),
    FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, order_id)
        REFERENCES orders(tenant_id, order_id)
);
~~~

This table is **not the customer's current contact profile**. Updating a customer's default address must never retroactively mutate the address snapshotted for a past order.

Moving the snapshot to its own table also **does not protect PII** if every role can still `SELECT` from it. Apply dedicated permissions, scoped APIs, retention policies, and log/backup protections.

### 6.3. Evidenced Historical Backfill

Suppose legacy `orders` has **`recipient_name`**, **`recipient_phone`**, **`address_line1`**, and **`city`** captured at confirmation. Once verified to be true confirmation snapshots, backfill:

~~~sql
INSERT INTO order_delivery_snapshots (
    tenant_id,
    order_id,
    recipient_name,
    recipient_phone,
    address_line1,
    city,
    captured_at
)
SELECT
    tenant_id,
    order_id,
    recipient_name,
    recipient_phone,
    address_line1,
    city,
    confirmed_at
FROM orders
WHERE recipient_name IS NOT NULL
  AND address_line1 IS NOT NULL
  AND city IS NOT NULL
ON CONFLICT (tenant_id, order_id) DO NOTHING;
~~~

**`ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING`** prevents duplicate keys on rerun, yet **does not prove conflicting rows share identical content**. If an existing snapshot and source differ, classify why: valid post-confirmation edit, load bug, mutated legacy row, or late update.

If legacy `orders` actually stored **live address synced from the customer profile**, it is **not proof of the address at confirmation**. Never rename columns and claim history is preserved. Use historical sources, acceptance logs, shipping manifests, or mark them **unverifiable**.

### 6.4. Switching Write Paths Without Creating Two Owners

During transition, both structures coexist. Specify:

- **Order confirmation** is the authoritative point deciding the snapshotted address.
- Within the same PostgreSQL instance, `INSERT` order and snapshot **in one transaction** if both must exist before confirmation succeeds.
- If snapshots live in another service/database, design **intermediate workflow states, idempotency, reliable events, and error handling**; never claim two cross-service writes are atomic.
- Legacy apps unaware of the new table must be upgraded or bridged compatibly before blocking old columns.
- Switch new readers only after completeness reconciliation; retire old readers only once unused.

A common path is **dual-read comparison** or **shadow-read**, shifting consumers one by one. Never let staff edit legacy address columns while the new service independently edits snapshots without conflict rules.

### 6.5. When Can Old Columns Be Dropped?

Only after:

1. All writers use the new path.
2. Apps, reports, exports, jobs, CDC, and caches no longer read the old columns.
3. Data is verified for **every shippable order**.
4. Old snapshots obey appropriate retention and access controls.
5. Rollbacks remain feasible or their support window has expired.
6. Owning teams sign off on the irreversible step.

**A table with fewer columns is not automatically a better model.** The value lies in clearer ownership, grain, and access boundaries.

## 7. Entity Splitting Is More Than Column Normalization

### 7.1. 1:1 Split for Lifecycle or Access Boundaries

For example, **`order_delivery_snapshots`** has 1 snapshot per order and splits 1:1 because many order consumers **do not need address PII**, and snapshots carry distinct permissions/retention.

Conversely, if attributes are always read/written together, share permissions, stay small, and share lifecycles, splitting tables adds JOIN and write overhead without improving the model. **There is no rule saying "every column group must become its own table".**

### 7.2. 1:N Split When an Entity Truly Has Multiple Versions or Items

A table **`products(product_id, sku, name, color, size, stock)`** cannot model one product having many SKUs. One split is:

~~~text
catalog_products
  product_id               -- grain: one product group
  product_name

product_variants
  variant_id               -- grain: one SKU/variant
  product_id               -- FK to catalog_products
  sku
  color, size

inventory_balances
  variant_id, warehouse_id -- grain: one SKU at one warehouse
  on_hand_quantity
~~~

If **`product_id`** previously meant SKU and now means product group, that is an **ID semantic change**, not just moving columns. Historical order lines must know **the exact SKU purchased**, never arbitrarily repointed to a generic product group that loses color/size.

Store and reconcile a **`legacy_product_id → variant_id`** mapping. Never default to the first variant when multiple valid variants exist.

### 7.3. Service-Boundary Ownership Splits

When Payment and Order have separate lifecycles, splitting tables inside one DB can be step one; moving to separate databases adds transaction and operational boundaries. During transition:

- Specify **who owns writes for each fact**.
- Never let two services independently `UPDATE` **`payment_status`**.
- Use versioned contracts/events to propagate derived states when needed.
- Keep capture/refund events in the authoritative source rather than inferring collected cash from `order.status`.
- Guard against duplicate writes, handle retries, and compensate cross-service failures.

**Splitting code or services does not eliminate business invariants**; it requires expressing those invariants through explicit protocols.

## 8. Example 3 — Entity Merge: Two Records, Same Customer?

Suppose CRM imports created **`C-101`** and **`C-202`** in the same tenant. Someone proposes:

~~~sql
-- DO NOT run as a generic merge pattern.
UPDATE orders
SET customer_id = 'C-101'
WHERE customer_id = 'C-202';

DELETE FROM customers
WHERE customer_id = 'C-202';
~~~

Even with a tenant filter, this script ignores critical questions:

- Were both accounts **verified to belong to the exact same subject**?
- Must **historical orders/invoices retain the customer ID used at transaction time**?
- Do the two accounts have different **permissions, passwords, MFA, consents, and payment profiles**?
- Should both accounts access the same orders post-merge?
- Do loyalty, rewards, coupons, returns, support, search, and analytics follow the same rule?
- Is **un-merge** needed if a merge turns out wrong?
- Does **`C-202`** still appear in external API contracts or legacy events?

One person owning two accounts **does not automatically grant cross-access to all orders**. Merging profiles or access is a **business and security decision** requiring approval, not just an FK `UPDATE`.

### 8.1. Distinguish Three Easily Conflated Cases

| Scenario | Should You Merge? | Actions Potentially Needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two records are duplicates of **the exact same entity** from a bad import | Possibly, after verification | Pick canonical, add alias, migrate links per policy |
| One person owns **two valid separate accounts** | Not necessarily | Optional owner/account link with explicit permissions |
| Two legal entities/units **merged corporately** | Not a simple ID merge | Legal history, effective dates, rights & obligations |
| One product changed SKU code | Usually alias/code change, not product merge | Keep SKU history and variant references |
| Two addresses share identical text | Insufficient proof of same person/business site | Normalize address; share only if appropriate |

**Merge criteria rest on entity identity and invariants**, never on sharing an email, name, or phone number—those attributes change, are shared, or are mistyped.

## 9. Designing Canonical IDs and Alias Tables Instead of Deleting History

On `customers(tenant_id, customer_id)`, add a mapping table:

~~~sql
CREATE TABLE customer_merge_aliases (
    tenant_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
    alias_customer_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    canonical_customer_id TEXT NOT NULL,
    decision_ref TEXT NOT NULL,
    merged_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
    PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, alias_customer_id),
    FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, alias_customer_id)
        REFERENCES customers (tenant_id, customer_id),
    FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, canonical_customer_id)
        REFERENCES customers (tenant_id, customer_id),
    CHECK (alias_customer_id <> canonical_customer_id)
);
~~~

In the example:

~~~text
tenant 17:
  C-202 -> C-101    (verified merge decision M-987)
  C-101 -> C-101    (implicitly canonical, no alias row needed)
~~~

A lookup function resolves alias IDs to canonical IDs under the **governed mapping**. However, this schema alone only enforces:

- Each alias has **one target** at a time (`PRIMARY KEY`).
- Alias and canonical belong to the same tenant with existing customer rows (`FK`).
- An alias cannot point directly to itself (`CHECK`).

It **does not prevent chains `C-202 → C-101 → C-500`** or cycles **`C-101 → C-202 → C-101`**, nor does it stop a canonical ID from being deleted/locked later. Implement transactional logic that **points aliases only to terminal canonical IDs**, blocks making a canonical an alias while dependents exist (or flattens chains cleanly), traverses graphs/recursively checks cycles, and locks involved accounts in deterministic order—never assume FKs prevent cycles.

### 9.1. An Alias Table Is Not Permission to Share Access

Once duplicates are verified, you may decide:

- API lookups on old IDs return **`canonical public ID`** per policy.
- Historical orders keep the original **`customer_id`** for document traceability.
- Current UI aggregates orders via aliases **only when access permissions are explicitly established**.
- The customer domain separates **account login**, **customer profile**, and **legal party** if needed.

Never let logins to one account automatically read all orders of another merely because an alias exists. Evaluate **authorization, consent, and data access** independently of canonicalization.

### 9.2. Controlled Merge Steps

1. **Verify same identity** using trusted evidence and approvals.
2. **Freeze conflicting writes** in scope and lock involved identifiers when updating mappings.
3. **Choose canonical ID** by policy (e.g., richest valid history, not blindly lowest ID).
4. **Record `decision_ref`, approver/method, timestamp**, and reviewability.
5. **Create alias** while preventing illegal cycles/chains.
6. **Resolve conflicting attributes field-by-field**: verified email, phone, address, consent, loyalty, and fraud flags follow distinct rules.
7. **Decide which links transfer**: profile views, login permissions, order history, payment methods, support tickets.
8. **Recompute projections/analytics** if distinct customer counts shift, recording effective time.
9. **Run security checks**: verify no unauthorized order, address, or payment exposure.
10. **Retain legacy aliases** until all consumers migrate and retention rules permit retirement.

Every merge must assess **blast radius and reversibility**. If a merge proves mistaken later, keeping original IDs on orders plus an audited mapping is far easier to undo than overwriting every historical trace.

## 10. Attribute Merging: Conflict Resolution Must Be Field-by-Field

Suppose canonical and alias profiles hold:

| Attribute | C-101 | C-202 | Rule Needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Display name | An Nguyễn | Nguyễn Văn An | Prefer verified source or preserve history—never blindly pick longest string |
| Email | `an@example.test` (verified) | `old@example.test` | Active email policy, verification state, consent |
| Phone number | From last year | Newly verified | Verification timestamp and usage rights |
| Default address | Hanoi | Da Nang | Never conflate with **locked order shipping address** |
| Loyalty points | 100 | 50 | Compute from points ledger; prevent double-counting |
| Refund/chargeback | Yes | No | Never erase risk flags during merge |
| Marketing consent | Yes | No | Never default to "Yes" just because one side opted in |

These are illustrative; **no universal "latest wins" rule fits every field**. "Latest" might mean data-entry time rather than business effective time. Consent and permissions follow strict policies—never escalate privileges via a merge.

### 10.1. Never `SUM` Two Balances If Events Overlap

Two loyalty profiles might include the same reward event imported from a third-party system. Blindly summing **`balance 100 + balance 50 = 150`** without reconciling the underlying ledger creates phantom points.

Define the **loyalty-points fact grain** as one earn/burn event with source ID, deduplicate overlapping events, and recompute canonical balance when permitted. Similarly, **`COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id)`** may legitimately drop from 2 to 1 post-merge, while order counts and monetary totals must never multiply or vanish from alias joins.

### 10.2. Merge As Of Which Point in Time?

If two accounts merged in November and the business runs a January unique-customers report:

- **Reporting by current identity:** may canonicalize past transactions to the current customer if business rules permit.
- **Reporting by identity known in January:** may keep two distinct identities since the merge decision did not exist yet.
- **Audit reporting:** must show when the merge decision was recorded and when it became effective.

An unversioned alias table answers only **current canonical identity**, insufficient to reconstruct past knowledge. If historical decision queries matter, store **effective time** and **recorded time** (or audit events) rather than overwriting alias rows in place.

## 11. Common Migration Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Is Dangerous | Prevention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Renaming a column and calling it a key migration | FKs, consumers, and uniqueness still follow old structure | Add new key, reconcile, shift references cleanly |
| Using **`CASCADE`** to drop a PK with live FKs | Can silently drop essential integrity constraints | Inventory dependencies; migrate FKs first |
| One-shot backfill ignoring concurrent writes | Leaves new rows keyless and mappings drifted | Transitional writer protocol + checkpoints + final reconciliation |
| Generating new IDs on every retry | One entity gets multiple IDs after reprocessing | Idempotency, unique business keys, durable mappings |
| Allowing independent edits to old and new columns | Two conflicting sources of truth | One-way derivation; mismatch checks |
| Merging solely on matching email/phone | Contacts are shared, changed, or mistyped | Evidenced identity resolution and sign-off |
| Overwriting `orders.customer_id` across all history | Loses which original account placed the transaction | Preserve original reference + alias/canonical view |
| Copying all PII when splitting tables | Multiplies copies and exposure surface | Data minimization, purpose-scoped access |
| Switching read paths before FK/data completeness | APIs return wrong or missing orders | Shadow reads, mismatch monitoring, phased cutover |
| Dropping old source right after deploy | Zero rollback; legacy consumers break | Expand–migrate–contract with retirement gates |
| Checking only `COUNT(*)` | One dropped row and one duplicated row cancel out | Compare keys, relations, payloads, and measure sums |
| Computing money from un-converged replicas | Decisions made on eventually stale data | Query authoritative source for strict invariants |

In particular, **"the schema runs" does not mean "the data is right"**. A valid FK pointing to an existing row can still point to the wrong **customer, tenant, temporal version, or owner**.

## 12. Transactions, Online Migration, and Operational Locks

### 12.1. Not Every DDL Is Safe During Peak Traffic

Techniques like **adding nullable columns without expensive defaults**, **`CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY`**, and **`VALIDATE CONSTRAINT`** reduce disruption, yet true online behavior depends on PostgreSQL version, table size, locks, workload, and active long queries.

Before executing:

- Measure row counts, write rates, table/index sizes, and tenant skew.
- Identify whether statements take **`ACCESS EXCLUSIVE`** or weaker locks, wait queues, and blocking risk.
- Configure **`lock_timeout`**, **`statement_timeout`**, and batching per system policy.
- Test on large datasets under concurrent writes—never just a 20-row table.
- Watch WAL, replication lag, disk headroom, autovacuum, and long transactions.
- Prepare pause/resume if a backfill fails without duplicating data.

**Never run a huge list of `ALTER TABLE` statements as if it were a free transaction.** For high availability, split into compatible phases as in Section 3.

### 12.2. Coexisting Application Versions

During rolling deployments, older instances remain active. Maintain a compatibility table:

| App Version | Reads `customer_id` | Writes `customer_id` | Reads `customer_sk` | Writes `customer_sk` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Legacy v1 | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| Transition v2 | Yes | Yes | Yes, explicit fallback | Yes, from mapping |
| New v3 | Compatibility only | While required | Yes | Yes |
| Post-contract | Per remaining legacy needs | Per policy | Yes | Yes |

While v1 still writes without populating `customer_sk`, **do not add `NOT NULL` on `customer_sk`** without a valid bridge. Defer the constraint, upgrade all writers first, or use a safe DB sync bridge during transition.

**Fallback reads must never mask bugs forever.** Instrument every fallback from `customer_sk` to `customer_id` with an expiration date—otherwise migrations linger half-finished for years.

## 13. APIs, Events, Caches, and Analytics Must Also Evolve

### 13.1. APIs: Stable External Contracts

Suppose an API returns:

~~~json
{
  "orderId": "O-9001",
  "customerRef": "c32b27d1-6737-4d63-ae83-7f8c0cb1c912",
  "status": "CONFIRMED",
  "total": {
    "amount": "450000.00",
    "currency": "VND"
  }
}
~~~

The API **does not have to expose `customer_sk`** when the DB adopts `BIGINT`. Internal mappers switch lookups while keeping `customerRef` stable. If public schemas truly must change, use versioned compatibility contracts with consumers rather than leaking new physical column names.

### 13.2. Events and CDC: Never Emit Physical Refactors as New Business Events

An event:

~~~json
{
  "eventId": "5f7cbd41-38c6-41eb-8205-8be22a57fe41",
  "eventType": "OrderConfirmed",
  "schemaVersion": 1,
  "orderId": "O-9001",
  "customerRef": "c32b27d1-6737-4d63-ae83-7f8c0cb1c912",
  "occurredAt": "2026-11-22T03:00:00Z"
}
~~~

...should not suddenly switch to emitting **`customer_sk`** merely because the DB added a column. Table-level **CDC** changes shape on renames/splits/merges, impacting consumers directly reading CDC rows. A stable integration-event mapping layer insulates downstream consumers from storage refactors.

When older events carry legacy `customer_id`, consumers may need **versioned alias resolution**. Support retries, out-of-order delivery, and idempotency; never re-emit duplicate **`OrderConfirmed`** events for an order just because keys are being backfilled.

### 13.3. Caches and Search Indexes

Cache keys like **`customer:C-101:orders`** become stale when canonical is `C-202`, yet invalidating and rebuilding caches must respect authorization. If two identities previously had different visibility, **merging caches** can leak data.

Projections and search indexes need:

- Source/canonical versions or checkpoints.
- Clean rebuilds from verified sources.
- Alias/redirect handling and dual-key invalidation where appropriate.
- Zero cross-account PII leaks simply because two IDs merged.
- Protection against stale events overwriting post-FK-migration state.

### 13.4. Warehouse and SCD Type 2

In a data warehouse, **`customer_key`** may be the surrogate key of a **dimension version** at transaction time—distinct from OLTP **`customer_sk`**. Never reuse numbers across both systems as identical keys without an explicit contract.

When merging customers:

- **`COUNT(DISTINCT customer)`** can shift under current canonical definitions.
- **Revenue, order counts, and item quantities** must never change due to ID remapping.
- Reports must clarify whether grouping uses **customer at purchase time** or **current canonical**.
- Retroactively updating dimension history or fact FKs requires restating and reconciling published aggregates.

**Never use a key migration as an excuse to rewrite locked prices, delivery addresses, order statuses, or event timestamps.**

## 14. Post-Split and Post-Merge Reconciliation: Verify Both Grain and Meaning

### 14.1. After Splitting `order_delivery_snapshots`

Suppose the business rule states **every confirmed physical order must have one delivery snapshot**. Once classification columns exist, find missing snapshots:

~~~sql
SELECT o.tenant_id, o.order_id
FROM orders AS o
LEFT JOIN order_delivery_snapshots AS d
  ON d.tenant_id = o.tenant_id
 AND d.order_id = o.order_id
WHERE o.order_type = 'PHYSICAL'
  AND o.order_status = 'CONFIRMED'
  AND d.order_id IS NULL;
~~~

This is an **illustrative query after extending the schema with `order_type`/`order_status`**; those columns were omitted from Section 4's minimal DDL. Never run the query verbatim on the demo schema without those fields. If orders have multiple shipments or different snapshot rules, adjust invariants accordingly.

Also verify **field content** where both old and new data coexist, not just row counts; where addresses were legitimately edited, track which revision is current.

### 14.2. After Merging, Keep Transaction Facts Unchanged

Before-and-after order-measure reconciliation:

~~~sql
-- Run over identical period, authoritative orders source, and currency.
SELECT
    tenant_id,
    currency,
    COUNT(*) AS order_count,
    SUM(confirmed_amount) AS confirmed_order_value
FROM orders
GROUP BY tenant_id, currency;
~~~

Compare two consistent snapshots of the **authoritative source**, grouped by tenant/currency and time period. Canonicalizing identity alone should **leave `order_count` and `confirmed_order_value` unchanged**. If they change, orders were dropped/duplicated or alias joins fanned out.

For **distinct customer counts**, numbers may **intentionally change** post-merge. Publish two explicit metrics:

- **`distinct_source_accounts`**: counts source accounts by original ID.
- **`distinct_canonical_customers`**: counts customers via approved canonical mappings as of contract time.

Never claim all dashboards stay identical post-merge, and never blindly accept every dashboard shift as valid. **Know which metrics are allowed to change.**

### 14.3. Mandatory Security Checks

- Zero new FKs pointing to customers in **another tenant**.
- No request gains read access to another account's orders solely via an alias without proper authorization.
- Retired legacy IDs are never reissued across invalid periods.
- Tokens, contacts, and consents are never merged via implicit defaults.
- External APIs/events/search/exports do not leak `customer_sk` or newly exposed sensitive fields.
- Users restricted/erased under privacy policy are never **resurrected** by backfills or historical replays.

**Data integrity and privacy are parallel test suites.** A migration with valid FKs that widens access beyond authorized boundaries still fails acceptance.

## 15. Rollback and Forward-Fix for Hard-to-Reverse Changes

| Situation | Viable Recovery | Prerequisites |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Added `customer_sk`, not yet cut over | Stop new writers; revert reads to old key | Old keys/FKs intact; sync verified |
| Switched readers while still writing old + new keys | Point reads back to v1 | Telemetry, mappings, no ID semantic drift |
| Dropped old key and stopped storing legacy ID | Code/data rollback difficult | Backups, durable mapping, forward-fix plan |
| Split snapshot, yet new table has bugs | Fall back to old source if still complete | Shadow comparisons, versions, dual-read |
| Deleted old PII column per policy | Never blindly restore from backup | Recovery process respecting erasure/retention |
| Mis-merged two customers with alias/audit kept | Revoke/invert decision; fix auth & read models | Merge log, original links, security tests |
| Mis-merged, rewrote all documents, deleted source | Clean split may be impossible | Avoid irreversible steps early; audit/backups |

**Rollbacks must never violate data erasure/restriction requests.** Restoring an old backup to revert a schema can resurrect revoked contacts or permissions. Reapply valid data-protection decisions before serving restored data.

Often **forward-fix** is superior: keep the new schema, repair mappings or restate bad rows, and reconcile. Choosing rollback vs. forward-fix depends on what remains reversible without breaking invariants.

## 16. Comparing Impact Across Relational, Document, Graph, and Analytics

| Model | Key Migration | Entity Split | Entity Merge |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Relational** | PK/UK/FK, indexes, transactions, constraint validation | 1:1, 1:N, FKs, permissions, transactions | Canonical IDs, aliases, historical FKs, uniqueness |
| **Document** | **`_id`** deeply coupled to reads/writes and references | Extract embedded subdocuments to collections or vice versa | Documents holding old ID refs & embedded snapshots |
| **Graph** | Node/edge identity and traversal references | Introduce new node types, semantic edges | Merging nodes easily multiplies/loses edges, relations, and time |
| **Time-series** | Sensor/device IDs across billions of events | Separate device metadata from raw samples | Never merge series from two sensors if distinct instruments |
| **Analytics** | Fact/dimension surrogate keys, lineage | Fact grain / dimension history may shift | Distinct counts, SCDs, retroactive restatements |

### 16.1. Document Models

If an Order is a document with public **`_id`** and embedded **`items[]`**, switching from sequential numbers to UUIDs is no simple PK swap: update referencing documents, events, indexes, caches, and clients storing `_id`.

A safer path **keeps the old ID as an alias**, adds a stable public ID field, builds new read projections, and shifts readers in phases. Extracting **`shippingAddress`** from Order documents requires decisions on snapshots, atomicity, and access controls—splitting collections alone does not guarantee security.

### 16.2. Graph Models

Merging two **`Customer`** nodes funnels **`PLACED_ORDER`**, **`OWNS_ACCOUNT`**, and **`BELONGS_TO`** edges onto one node. If an edge represents "has purchased", merging must not collapse multiple transaction edges into one and lose event counts, timestamps, or edge properties. Preserve **event identity** rather than deduping parallel edges with matching endpoints when each represents a separate transaction.

When graphs drive authorization, merging nodes can **widen allowed traversal paths**, creating security holes unless access policies are re-verified.

### 16.3. Analytics and SCDs

When products split into product/variant, sales line facts must point to the **variant at sale time** to preserve color/size analysis. Merging two current product groups must never erase **SKU-level distinction on invoices**.

In Type 2 dimensions, changing natural keys need not change historical surrogate keys. A canonical merge may use an additional bridge/mapping table instead of rewriting every fact key. Every metric shift must declare **grain, effective time, and recomputation policy**.

## 17. Designing an Executable Migration Plan

Pre-rollout decision template:

| Item | Example Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Reason for change** | Legacy IDs recycled; need delivery boundary; CRM duplicates |
| **Authorities** | Customer Domain, Order Domain, Identity team |
| **Before/after grain** | One account/tenant; one snapshot/order; one alias→canonical |
| **Keys and scope** | `(tenant, customer_id)`, `customer_sk`, `public_customer_id` |
| **Compatibility structures** | Old columns, new columns, aliases, v1/v2 views |
| **Writers** | Which app versions write in each phase; who enforces mappings |
| **Readers/consumers** | APIs, batches, brokers, CDC, warehouse, caches, partners |
| **History rules** | Do orders/prices/documents keep original IDs or time-aware canonicals? |
| **Backfill** | Batch size, checkpoints, retries, unmappable-data handling |
| **Constraints** | Unique, FK, `NOT NULL`, tenant scope, cycle guards, permissions |
| **Acceptance metrics** | `NULL=0`, `orphan=0`, `mismatch=0`, read/write p95 and lag |
| **Cutover** | Per-tenant/canary, shadow reads, feature flags |
| **Rollback/forward-fix** | Reversible point; sources kept for recovery |
| **Contract cleanup** | Sunset date for old IDs/fields; proof of zero consumers |

Reliable model changes rely on **verifiable metrics**. "Deploy succeeded" or "script returned 0 errors" is never sufficient.

### 17.1. Gate Before Switching Writers/Readers

- New writes generate complete new keys without cross-tenant FKs.
- Backfill meets approved thresholds across all tenants; unmappable cases resolved explicitly.
- Required FKs/unique/check constraints validated against the new model.
- Shadow reads return identical **public IDs, statuses, prices, and permissions** per contract.
- External services using versioned events/APIs tested without coupling to physical PKs.
- Rollback or forward-fix paths rehearsed and timed.
- PII permissions, caches/analytics, and retention verified.
- Monitoring, on-call coverage, and cutover abort criteria ready.

### 17.2. Gate Before Dropping Legacy Structures

- Zero readers/writers rely on old keys/fields, or approved adapters remain.
- Zero legacy events/queues/backfills can write stale values backward.
- Zero dependent FKs, views, triggers, indexes, jobs, and reports remain.
- Full or approved partial reconciliation passes with recorded evidence.
- No valid reason remains to retain legacy IDs outside protected metadata/audits.
- Drop steps rehearsed against backups, restores, locks, and recovery checks.
- Owners and consumers confirm retirement of the transition contract.

### Exercise 1 — Surrogate Key vs. Business Key

**Question:** What is flawed in that conclusion? What checks/constraints are needed before full cutover?

### Exercise 3 — Dual-Write Produces Wrong Mapping

Order O-9 has `customer_id = C-1` yet `customer_sk` points to `C-2` in the same tenant. Both customers exist, so the new FK passes.

**Question:** Why didn't the FK catch this semantic bug? Write the reconciliation rule comparing both keys.

### Exercise 4 — Splitting Addresses When an Order Has Multiple Parcels

**Question:** Is grain **`(tenant_id, order_id)`** sufficient for delivery snapshots? What entity/key is needed?

### Exercise 6 — Merging by Email

**Question:** What identity and security bugs occurred? What gates are needed before merging?

### Exercise 7 — Cyclic Aliases

A mapping table holds `A → B`, `B → C`, and a job inserts `C → A`. All of A, B, and C exist with valid FKs.

**Question:** Why is this structure invalid? How do you prevent cycles without relying solely on FKs?

### Exercise 8 — Loyalty Balance Post-Merge

**Question:** Can you sum `100 + 50`? Which fact model should be reconciled?

### Exercise 9 — Reports Shift After Merge

After merging two accounts, **`COUNT(DISTINCT customer)`** drops from 2 to 1, and **`SUM(confirmed_amount)`** also drops 20%.

**Question:** Which shift can be valid and which demands investigation?

### Exercise 10 — Rollback After Dropping Legacy Key

A team dropped `customer_id` from `orders`, then discovered an old consumer still requires it while a full mapping table is missing.

**Question:** Why is simple rollback impossible? What gate should have preceded `contract`?

### Exercise 11 — Migrating Across Coexisting App Versions

Some API v1 instances still write only `customer_id`, while API v2 writes both keys. The team adds `NOT NULL (customer_sk)` right after deploying v2.

**Question:** What is the risk and what transitional options exist?

### Exercise 12 — Time-Effective Corporate Merger

Two companies merge on July 1, yet June invoices still belong to the old legal entity. A team updates all historical FKs to the new company code.

**Question:** Is this a simple duplicate merge? How should time and relationships be modeled?

## 19. Key Takeaways

**Primary key migration** preserves the mapping between old and new identifiers—and every relationship—while writers and readers transition in phases. **Entity splitting** redefines grain and lifecycle boundaries, not just column placement. **Entity merging** is a business decision about whether two records share one identity, paired with authorization and historical rules—never just an FK `UPDATE`.

In every case, **schema changes must never rewrite transaction facts**: locked prices, per-shipment delivery addresses, payment captures, and event timestamps must retain exact meaning. Derived views can be rebuilt; historical records must be protected explicitly.

Before running a migration, ask: **"What does a new ID represent, which references must move, when must old IDs stay to explain history, who is authorized post-merge, and what evidence proves zero lost or duplicated facts?"**. Answering with contracts, constraints, reconciliation, and recovery plans allows models to evolve while staying trustworthy.
