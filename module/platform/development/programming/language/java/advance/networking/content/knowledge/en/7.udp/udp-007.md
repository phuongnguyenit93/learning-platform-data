# Packet Loss, Duplication, Reordering, and Limitations of Sender-Side Error Reporting in UDP

## 1. Core Answer

**UDP (User Datagram Protocol)** provides a service for sending/receiving **independent datagrams** between IP/port endpoints. It preserves the **boundary of a completely received datagram**, but it **does not guarantee** that the datagram will be delivered to the destination, does not automatically retransmit lost datagrams, does not prevent duplicate reception, does not restore ordering, and does not provide ACK/flow control/congestion control mechanisms like TCP.

The most important point of this topic: **`DatagramSocket.send(packet)` returning normally is not proof that the receiving application has received or processed the packet**. In the typical case, it only indicates that the send operation was accepted by Java/the OS at the level observable by the API. The packet can still be lost in buffers, network interface cards, routers, firewalls, NATs, the transmission path, the receiving OS, or application processing. Even an ICMP error report may never reach the sending process, arrive late, or fail to identify which application packet failed.

Scenarios a UDP programmer must account for:

| Phenomenon | What the sender may see | What the receiver may see | Does UDP resolve it automatically? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Datagram loss** | `send()` may still succeed | No corresponding packet | **No** |
| **Datagram duplication** | May have sent only once, or the program retries itself | Receives multiple datagrams carrying the same logical message | **No** |
| **Reordering** | Calls `send` in order A → B → C | May receive B before A | **No** |
| **Excessive delay** | `send()` has already returned | Packet arrives after the processing deadline | **No** |
| **No listening process at destination** | May or may not have an error; sometimes receives ICMP/`PortUnreachableException` | No receiving application at that port | **No ACK from UDP** |
| **Datagram exceeds limit / rejected locally** | `send()` may throw `IOException`/`SocketException` | Usually no valid packet reaches the destination | May report **some local errors**, does not guarantee reporting all path errors |

A `DatagramSocket` differs from a TCP `Socket`: **there is no handshake step proving the peer is listening**, no continuous byte stream, and **no `-1` EOF** when the peer stops operating.

## 2. Four Phenomena on the Same Sequence

Suppose the sender intends to send four messages with sequence numbers **0, 1, 2, 3**. The receiver application observes **0, 2, 2, 1**; meanwhile, 3 does not appear before the receive cycle deadline.

~~~text
Sender intends:    0 ─────► 1 ─────► 2 ─────► 3
                           │        │        │
Transmission path:         │        ├─ duplicated └─ lost before receiver
                           │        │
Receiver observes:  0 ───► 2 ───► 2 ───► 1
                    OK     gap 1    dup 2    1 arrives late
~~~

Analytical implications:

- When receiving **2 before 1**, the receiver can see a **gap**, but **cannot yet conclude that 1 is permanently lost**. It may arrive later due to reordering, network delay, or an application-layer retransmission mechanism.
- When receiving **2 a second time** within the same session, it is a **duplicate by logical key** if the protocol specifies that sequence 2 is unique. The receiver must choose to discard it, re-acknowledge it, or process it idempotently.
- When receiving **1 after 2**, 1 is **out of order** relative to the expected order; send order does not dictate delivery order to the application.
- When the deadline expires without seeing **3**, it can be called **missing/loss according to the chosen observation window and deadline**. There is no mathematical proof that packet 3 will not appear afterward.

For a protocol without a sequence ID, the receiver may **be unable to distinguish** whether two packets carrying the same payload are a network duplicate or two valid events with identical data; nor does it have a basis to determine which packet was lost. To detect these, the protocol must carry identifying information up front.

## 3. Where Does Packet Loss Occur?

UDP does not retransmit automatically. A packet may fail to reach the receiver for many reasons, not just because of a “weak Internet connection”.

| Location | Typical cause | What the application observes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sender: queue/OS** | Insufficient buffer, sending rate exceeds resources, kernel limits | Send may report an error or succeed even though the packet does not go far |
| **Transmission path / network devices** | Congestion, noise, physical errors, router drops | No mandatory UDP notification |
| **NAT/firewall/load balancer** | ACLs, expired UDP mapping, route changes, packet-drop policies | Sender usually sees no specific ACK/error |
| **IP fragmentation / MTU** | A lost fragment prevents reassembly of the UDP datagram | Receiver does not receive the complete datagram |
| **Receiver side: socket queue** | App calls `receive` slowly, receive queue full, CPU bottleneck | Packet is dropped before the app reads it |
| **Application side** | Packet rejected by parser, wrong session, expired, failed authentication | Socket may receive it, but logic does not count it as successfully processed |

**Where packets are lost and why it cannot be inferred solely from the result of `send()`.** If sequence numbers are present, the missing rate can be measured at the application; to isolate the location, one must also measure socket drops, NIC statistics, sending and receiving hosts, firewall/router counters, and packet captures at multiple points.

UDP has a checksum to help detect data corruption at the transport layer; a packet with a bad checksum may be discarded. The checksum **does not provide** ACKs, retransmission, ordering, or spoofing protection. In IPv6, the UDP checksum is normally mandatory (with specialized exceptions per standard); in IPv4, there are cases where a zero checksum is used, so the existence of a checksum should not be taken as proof that every environment will detect all errors.

## 4. Packet Duplication from the Network or from the Program?

UDP **does not guarantee duplicate elimination**. Although duplication on a normal network path is not the most common event, the protocol must handle that possibility. In practice, **application retries** are also a very common source of duplicates:

1. Client sends a state-transition request `requestId=42`.
2. Server receives and **processes it successfully**, then sends an ACK.
3. The ACK is lost; the client only sees a **timeout**.
4. Client retries the same request 42.
5. Server receives the request **a second time**; without deduplication, a non-idempotent operation could be executed twice.

Therefore, do not compare only the **payload content** to assert a duplicate: two customers sending the same text can still be two independent requests. Use an application key such as **`(sessionId, messageId)`** or **`(clientId, requestId)`**, along with an appropriate lifetime scope and identity binding.

Handling levels:

- **Best effort:** allows loss or duplication when acceptable to the application (high-frequency telemetry, transient state).
- **At-most-once processing:** deduplicates by ID, but a message may never be processed.
- **At-least-once attempts/delivery under the assumption of a working path and successful retries:** uses ACK + retry; duplicate handling must be addressed.
- **Exactly-once effect:** requires additional application mechanisms such as idempotency keys and persisting the result state **durably and atomically** together with the business effect; a single `send()` call and a single ACK do not guarantee this on their own.

A dedup cache living only in RAM will lose history after a restart; if this is a payment or critical update, it is usually necessary to persist the result by idempotency key in a database or appropriate transactional system.

## 5. Reordering and Numbering

With TCP, the byte stream is already ordered when the application reads it. UDP delivers **independent datagrams**. Datagrams may take different routes, experience different queues, or be delayed by the system; **the order of `send` calls does not constitute a guarantee of receive order**.

For example, the application sends:

~~~text
seq=100: x=1
seq=101: x=2
seq=102: x=3
~~~

The receiver may see `100, 102, 101`. If each packet is a **state snapshot**, it can simply apply the latest snapshot and discard older data; if each packet is an **event that must be processed in order**, the receiver must have a reorder buffer and a rule for “how long to wait before considering a packet missing”.

### Sequence Numbers Are Only a Detection Tool

A minimal header typically includes:

~~~text
version | sessionId | sequenceNumber | messageType | payloadLength | payload
~~~

- **`sessionId`** prevents confusing packets from an old session with a new session when the same address/port is reused.
- **`sequenceNumber`** is used to detect gaps, duplicates, old/late packets, and to reorder. Sequence numbers typically require wraparound rules if using finite integers.
- **`messageType`** specifies the message type; **`payloadLength`** helps validate framing at the application layer and detect truncated reads caused by an insufficient buffer.
- **Authenticity/integrity:** if the data is important, packets must be authenticated, and replay and sender origin must be checked; the UDP/IP source address alone is not trustworthy proof.

Example where the receiver already knows session S and is expecting `next=51`:

| Received | Initial meaning | Example action |
|---|---|---|
| `seq=51` | In order | Apply and increment `next` |
| `seq=53` | Missing 51/52 or currently reordered | Hold temporarily in reorder buffer; set deadline |
| `seq=52` after seeing 53 | Late packet, but potentially valid | Place in correct position; release contiguous packets |
| `seq=53` a second time | Duplicate if already processed/held in the same session | Discard payload, optionally re-ACK |
| `seq=45` | Old packet or wrapped-around number; policy must be known | Compare against sliding window and session, do not infer naively |

Do not use `seq < highestSeen` as the sole definition of **loss**: that only indicates out-of-order in a simple model. A sequence gap may close later; a duplicate packet may arrive long after the original. The reorder buffer must be bounded in **packet count, bytes, and time**; otherwise, an attacker or an unstable network can cause unbounded memory growth.

## 6. The Most Important Limitation: What Can `DatagramSocket.send()` Report?

### 6.1. Sending Successfully Does Not Mean “Delivered”

Java 17:

~~~java
// Excerpt; socket, payload, and destination are already prepared.
DatagramPacket packet = new DatagramPacket(
        payload, payload.length, destination);
socket.send(packet); // Returning normally is only the result of the local send operation.
~~~

`DatagramSocket.send(DatagramPacket)` is a method that **returns `void`**. It has no response object containing an ACK, no byte count received by the peer, and no ID confirming processing. On typical systems, send hands the datagram off to the local network stack; afterward, it can be dropped without any final notification to Java.

When an error is **detected during the send operation**, Java may throw `IOException` or subclasses of `SocketException`; for example, a closed socket, invalid arguments, permission issues, inability to send on the chosen route, or an unacceptable size. However, **the exact list of errors and when they are thrown depend on the OS/JDK**.

Separate three questions:

| Question | Does `send()` answer reliably? |
|---|---|
| Did Java invoke the send API and did the current operation complete without throwing an error? | **Yes**, within the scope of the API |
| Did the packet definitely leave the NIC and reach the destination host? | **No** |
| Did the destination application read, validate, and process it successfully? | **No** |

Even a packet that has reached **the receiving host's kernel** can still be dropped before `DatagramSocket.receive()`. To know that processing succeeded, you must design an **application-level ACK**, and the ACK itself can also be lost.

### 6.2. UDP `connect()` Is Not TCP `connect()`

~~~java
socket.connect(remoteAddress, remotePort);
socket.send(packet);
~~~

For UDP, `DatagramSocket.connect(...)` **binds the socket to a remote endpoint** so that it only sends/receives from that endpoint (and may help the OS report certain ICMP errors). It **does not** perform a TCP three-way handshake, does not establish an ACK exchange, and does not guarantee that any process has the remote port open.

This explains why a “connected” UDP socket can still send to a port with no listener and `send()` will sometimes still return normally.

### 6.3. `PortUnreachableException` and ICMP

Java provides `java.net.PortUnreachableException`, a subclass of `SocketException`, when receiving an **ICMP Port Unreachable**-style notification for a connected datagram socket. The Javadoc of `DatagramSocket.send()`/`receive()` explicitly states that this exception **may be thrown, but is not guaranteed**.

Reasons you cannot rely on this exception to detect all packet loss:

- Routers/NATs/firewalls may **silently drop** packets, block ICMP, or rate-limit ICMP responses.
- Not every failure is of the “no listener at destination port” kind; congestion drops, an overloaded receiver, or the app discarding the payload do not generate a useful ICMP Port Unreachable for the sender.
- ICMP may **arrive late**; the exception might surface on a subsequent `send` or `receive` call and does not precisely mean that the packet currently being sent/received is the failed one.
- How the OS delivers ICMP to the socket and how the JDK surfaces the error can vary; even a connected UDP socket is **not promised** to always throw `PortUnreachableException`.
- An ICMP response does not replace logical confirmation that **the application processed the message**; communication must be verified at the application layer.

Example of catching the exception only to **record a signal**, not treating it as a meter for “total lost packets”:

~~~java
// Excerpt, socket is UDP-connected to remoteAddress/remotePort.
try {
    socket.send(packet);
    // No error ≠ received successfully.
} catch (java.net.PortUnreachableException e) {
    // Unreachable signal provided by OS/JDK, not guaranteed every time.
} catch (java.io.IOException e) {
    // Handle I/O error observed by the application.
}
~~~

Do not write logic like “no PortUnreachableException seen within 100 ms ⇒ server is definitely up”. UDP has no end-to-end connection check in that manner.

### 6.4. Receiver Timeout Is Not Sender Error Reporting

~~~java
receiver.setSoTimeout(1_000); // 1 second
receiver.receive(packet);
~~~

If no matching datagram arrives within the wait time, `receive` throws `SocketTimeoutException`. It indicates that **the wait at the receiver** has expired; it does not prove that a specific packet was lost and does not automatically send an error report to the client. The datagram may arrive after the timeout.

`DatagramSocket.receive` returns `void` and populates `DatagramPacket`. **There is no `-1` meaning the peer closed the stream** like TCP's `InputStream.read()`. UDP has no FIN/EOF to signal that the peer has finished sending. A **zero-length UDP payload** is also a valid datagram; `packet.getLength() == 0` does not mean the connection is closed.

## 7. Runnable Java 17+ Example: Simulating Loss, Duplication, and Reordering

To observe this **in a controlled way with reproducible stdout**, the example creates **3 `DatagramSocket` instances on loopback** within **one JVM**:

1. **Sender** sends four packets carrying sequences `0, 1, 2, 3` to a relay and counts four `send` calls that return normally.
2. **Relay** collects the four packets, stores them by sequence, and then **intentionally forwards** them in the order `0, 2, 2, 1`: dropping `3`, sending `2` twice, and delivering `1` after `2`.
3. **Receiver** receives the datagrams, parses the sequence ID, and reports `NEW`, `GAP`, `DUPLICATE`, or `OUT_OF_ORDER`.

This is an **intentional simulation at the relay**, **not a claim** that the real loopback network suffered loss/duplication/reordering. It illustrates the sender-side limitation and what the application layer must handle when an intermediate network produces those conditions.

Create the file **`UdpDeliveryDemo.java`**:

~~~java
import java.io.IOException;
import java.net.DatagramPacket;
import java.net.DatagramSocket;
import java.net.InetAddress;
import java.net.InetSocketAddress;
import java.nio.ByteBuffer;
import java.util.Arrays;
import java.util.HashMap;
import java.util.Map;

public class UdpDeliveryDemo {
    public static void main(String[] args) throws Exception {
        InetAddress loopback = InetAddress.getLoopbackAddress();
        try (DatagramSocket sender = new DatagramSocket();
             DatagramSocket relay = new DatagramSocket(
                     new InetSocketAddress(loopback, 0));
             DatagramSocket receiver = new DatagramSocket(
                     new InetSocketAddress(loopback, 0))) {

            relay.setSoTimeout(3_000);
            receiver.setSoTimeout(3_000);
            InetSocketAddress relayAddress =
                    new InetSocketAddress(loopback, relay.getLocalPort());
            InetSocketAddress receiverAddress =
                    new InetSocketAddress(loopback, receiver.getLocalPort());

            int sendCalls = 0;
            for (int seq = 0; seq < 4; seq++) {
                byte[] payload = ByteBuffer.allocate(4).putInt(seq).array();
                sender.send(new DatagramPacket(
                        payload, payload.length, relayAddress));
                sendCalls++;
            }

            Map<Integer, byte[]> packets = new HashMap<>();
            for (int i = 0; i < 4; i++) {
                DatagramPacket packet = new DatagramPacket(new byte[64], 64);
                relay.receive(packet);
                if (packet.getLength() != 4) {
                    throw new IOException("Wrong payload size");
                }
                byte[] bytes = Arrays.copyOfRange(packet.getData(),
                        packet.getOffset(),
                        packet.getOffset() + packet.getLength());
                int seq = ByteBuffer.wrap(bytes).getInt();
                if (packets.put(seq, bytes) != null) {
                    throw new IOException("Unexpected duplicate at relay");
                }
            }

            System.out.println("sendCalls=" + sendCalls);
            int[] forwarded = {0, 2, 2, 1}; // Drop 3, duplicate 2, reorder 1.
            boolean[] seen = new boolean[4];
            int highest = -1;
            for (int seq : forwarded) {
                byte[] payload = packets.get(seq);
                relay.send(new DatagramPacket(
                        payload, payload.length, receiverAddress));

                DatagramPacket received = new DatagramPacket(new byte[64], 64);
                receiver.receive(received);
                if (received.getLength() != 4) {
                    throw new IOException("Truncated or invalid packet");
                }
                int number = ByteBuffer.wrap(received.getData(),
                        received.getOffset(), received.getLength()).getInt();
                if (number < 0 || number >= seen.length) {
                    throw new IOException("Unexpected sequence number");
                }

                String event;
                if (seen[number]) {
                    event = "DUPLICATE";
                } else if (number < highest) {
                    event = "OUT_OF_ORDER";
                } else if (number > highest + 1) {
                    event = "GAP";
                } else {
                    event = "NEW";
                }
                seen[number] = true;
                highest = Math.max(highest, number);
                System.out.println("seq=" + number + " event=" + event);
            }

            for (int i = 0; i < seen.length; i++) {
                if (!seen[i]) {
                    System.out.println("missingAtEnd=" + i);
                }
            }
        }
    }
}
~~~

Compile and run with **JDK 17+**:

~~~bash
javac --release 17 UdpDeliveryDemo.java
java UdpDeliveryDemo
~~~

**Verified stdout on OpenJDK 24 compiled with `--release 17`:**

~~~text
sendCalls=4
seq=0 event=NEW
seq=2 event=GAP
seq=2 event=DUPLICATE
seq=1 event=OUT_OF_ORDER
missingAtEnd=3
~~~

Accurate interpretation:

- `sendCalls=4` only counts **four `sender.send` calls that did not throw an error** to the relay. It **does not say that the final receiver processed all four**.
- `seq=2 event=GAP` only indicates that at that moment **1 had not been seen yet**; on the last line, 1 arrived so it is no longer missing.
- `seq=2 event=DUPLICATE` because the receiver **had already seen 2 in the same simulated dataset**. The demo only flags the duplicate; it does not yet implement business-level handling/durable deduplication.
- `seq=1 event=OUT_OF_ORDER` because 1 arrived after 2 had already appeared. The demo flags the phenomenon rather than **automatically restoring processing order**.
- `missingAtEnd=3` because the program **knows in advance that the expected sequence set is 0..3**, stops after four receives, and the relay intentionally dropped 3. A production receiver cannot know “it is finished” unless the protocol provides an expected range, END marker, deadline, or specific rule.

The statement `ByteBuffer.allocate(4).putInt(seq)` encodes the sequence number as **4 big-endian bytes**. The receiver uses `packet.getLength()` and `packet.getOffset()`, parsing only the exact portion of data received. For real packets, you also need version, session ID, length, size limits, and validity checks before accessing the array. `DatagramSocket` uses **port 0** so the OS assigns an ephemeral port, not to send to UDP port 0.

## 8. How to Design ACKs, Retries, and Timeouts

If the application protocol needs to know a message arrived, define an **ACK by message ID**, for example:

~~~text
Client                                  Server
  DATA(session=S, seq=42)  ------------> receive, validate, dedup, process
  <-----------------------  ACK(S, 42)  (ACK may be lost)
       wait for ACK up to a deadline
       if not received: may retry DATA(S,42), per policy
~~~

**A timeout waiting for an ACK has at least three interpretations:** (1) DATA was lost; (2) the server received DATA but the ACK was lost; (3) the server or network path is slow, and DATA/ACK is still in flight. Therefore, **“ACK timeout = DATA was definitely lost” is false**.

For applications requiring reliability:

- Use a **unique requestId within the session scope**; keep the same ID when retrying **the same operation**. Changing the request ID on retry may make the server think it is a new operation.
- **Receiver deduplicates and processes idempotently**; if a valid duplicate arrives after a lost ACK, return the ACK or stored result instead of re-executing the business effect.
- Have a **retry limit, exponential backoff, and jitter**; avoid retrying continuously when the network is congested. The retransmission mechanism needs congestion control rather than pushing unbounded traffic.
- Use an **overall deadline** on the client, based on a **monotonic clock** for elapsed intervals (e.g., `System.nanoTime()`), distinct from the deadline of a single `receive()` call.
- **What an ACK means must be clearly defined**: received by the socket, parsed, enqueued, or transaction committed? A “received” ACK does not automatically guarantee “durably processed”.
- Bound the number of messages awaiting ACK, retransmission volume, queue sizes, and reorder buffer bytes. Otherwise, a slow peer or spoofed packets can exhaust memory.
- For systems requiring complex ordered reliable delivery, **TCP** or a **QUIC stream** usually saves effort compared to reinventing a transport protocol over UDP. Note that **QUIC DATAGRAM** is also an unreliable datagram type with its own semantics.

### Why Doesn't “Exactly Once” Appear Automatically with ACKs?

Suppose the server **wrote the order to the database**, but crashed before sending the ACK. The client retries and a newly restarted server receives it; without a **durably stored idempotency key**, it could record a duplicate. Conversely, sending the ACK too early before committing can make the client believe the operation succeeded even if the server crashes and loses the work.

End-to-end reliability requires coordination rules across **receive/validate → process → commit → record ID → ACK** so they do not conflict upon crash/retry. UDP does not provide that business transaction.

## 9. Pitfalls When Receiving UDP in Java

### 9.1. UDP Preserves Datagram Boundaries, but Buffers Can Truncate the Tail

~~~java
// Excerpt at the receiver; socket is already bound:
byte[] buffer = new byte[512];
DatagramPacket packet = new DatagramPacket(buffer, buffer.length);
socket.receive(packet);

int length = packet.getLength();
int offset = packet.getOffset();
byte[] payload = java.util.Arrays.copyOfRange(
        packet.getData(), offset, offset + length);
~~~

**Each successful `receive` receives one datagram**, not “a contiguous chunk of a byte stream” like TCP. However, if the datagram is **larger than the capacity of the packet/buffer**, the data may **be truncated**; you cannot read “the remaining part of that same datagram” in a subsequent `receive` call.

If `getLength() == buffer.length`, it could be an exact-fit datagram or one truncated at the limit; **you cannot conclude** which one from this equality alone. The protocol should have an agreed-upon maximum payload and metadata to validate the length.

When **reusing the same `DatagramPacket` for multiple receives**, remember to call `packet.setLength(buffer.length)` before each read; after the previous receive, the length on the packet may have shrunk, causing a new datagram to be truncated to the old limit.

### 9.2. Timeouts, Empty Packets, and Closing the Socket

| Observation at receiver | Correct interpretation |
|---|---|
| `receive(packet)` succeeds, `getLength() > 0` | A datagram carrying the corresponding number of bytes arrived |
| `receive(packet)` succeeds, `getLength() == 0` | **An empty-payload UDP datagram** can be completely valid |
| `SocketTimeoutException` | `receive` wait time expired; does not confirm a specific packet is permanently lost |
| `SocketException` after local socket closes | Error due to local socket state; not a UDP EOF |
| Nothing received for several minutes | Could be no sender, packet loss, firewall, wrong port, overloaded app, etc.; cannot yet infer the cause |

Do not expect `receive` to return `-1` like `InputStream.read()` on TCP: it **does not return an integer**. If you want the peer to signal completion, define an **application-layer CLOSE/END message**, while also accounting for the possibility that this message itself may be lost.

### 9.3. Oversized Datagrams and IP Fragmentation

Theoretically, a UDP payload over IPv4 with a minimum 20-byte IP header and 8-byte UDP header can reach about **65,507 bytes**; this is **not** a safe practical size for all network paths. Actual MTU is usually much lower and can be even lower due to VPNs/tunnels.

Large UDP datagrams may undergo **IP fragmentation**; losing just one fragment can prevent **the entire datagram from being delivered to the UDP receiver**. For Internet applications, prefer small packets within the **effective Path MTU** or actively support MTU discovery/appropriate packetization. Do not treat “`send()` did not throw an error” as proof that fragments were reassembled on the receiving side.

If the protocol needs to transfer large payloads, you can choose TCP/QUIC streams or define your own splitting into multiple independent datagrams with IDs, ordering, size limits, integrity checks, and reassembly conditions.

## 10. Diagnosing Loss, Duplication, Reordering, and Send Errors

### Observe Across Multiple Layers, Not Just Sender Logs

| Metric / tool | What evidence it provides | What cannot be concluded |
|---|---|---|
| Counter of `send()` calls without errors | How many send operations returned normally | That the receiver processed them all |
| Receiver counter by `(sessionId, seq)` | Unique received, duplicates, gaps, late/out-of-order | Which exact network node caused the loss |
| ACK/commit log | Receiver confirmed at the application-defined level | That ACKs are never lost or the server never crashes |
| `SocketTimeoutException` at receiver | No datagram was delivered during the wait interval | That the datagram is permanently lost |
| `PortUnreachableException` | An unreachable signal was reported by OS/JDK for connected UDP | That all network errors are detected |
| Packet capture at both ends | Packets seen at capture points with timestamps | That the application definitely processed them successfully |
| NIC/kernel/socket drop counters | Drops occurred at the observed component | That all lost packets occurred at that component |

With Wireshark/tcpdump, filter by the specific UDP port and cross-reference **application sequence numbers**, timestamps, and source/destination. Synchronize timestamp conventions when comparing two hosts; `System.nanoTime()` can be used for **elapsed time within the same JVM**, but do not subtract `nanoTime` values from two different hosts directly.

Useful metrics typically include **send attempts**, **unique received**, **duplicates**, **out-of-order**, **still missing after reorder deadline**, **ACK latency**, **retry count**, **receive queue drops**, packet size, and rejected packet count. Application-level packet loss may not match NIC drop counts because packets can be dropped at another hop or by the processing logic itself.

### Scenarios Requiring the Right Questions

**Sender sees no exception; receiver sees no packet.** Check remote IP/port, firewall/NAT, IPv4/IPv6, socket bind address, packet capture, receive queue, and timeout. This is a normal UDP case that must be handled by application mechanisms.

**Receiver sees sequence 7 before seeing 6.** Record a gap and wait according to policy; do not immediately mark “6 is definitely lost” if the application tolerates reordering.

**Receiver sees two packets with the same payload.** Check session and request IDs before labeling them duplicates; identical payloads **do not necessarily** mean the same logical event.

**Sender sees `PortUnreachableException` once after multiple sends.** Do not automatically attribute that error specifically to “the last datagram definitely did not arrive”, because ICMP signals can be delivered late and are not guaranteed across all platforms.

**Receiver always times out when sender says it sent.** Do not automatically assume the sender is lying: the two sides may be observing different points along the datagram's path.

## 11. Security and Production Operations

UDP has no default handshake or identity authentication. A sender can craft packets with an untrusted source address in some network environments. Therefore, **sequence numbers or ACKs should only be trusted when bound to an authenticated peer/session**; otherwise, an attacker can inject spoofed packets, send fake ACKs, or fill up the dedup/reorder cache.

Limits are needed on **packet size**, session count, ingestion rate, memory for pending packets, and timeouts. ACK/response mechanisms must not reply unboundedly to unauthenticated sources due to the risk of **UDP reflection/amplification**. On congested paths, uncontrolled retries can worsen congestion.

A simple design for real-time state telemetry often accepts dropping old snapshots and keeping only the latest one; critical transactional systems usually require acknowledgments, idempotency, or switching to a reliable transport. **No single policy fits all data**.

## 12. Self-Check Exercises

**1. Sender calls `send()` successfully—has the UDP receiver definitely received the datagram?** **No.** The sender receives no delivery acknowledgment from UDP.

**2. Receiver sees 5, 7, 6. Was packet 6 ever lost?** Cannot be concluded. At the moment 7 was seen, 6 **had not appeared yet**; afterward 6 arrived late, exhibiting reordering.

**3. Receiver sees 5, 5. Did the network definitely duplicate the packet?** No. The client may have retransmitted due to a lost ACK, or they may be two valid messages with identical payloads. Session/request IDs must be checked.

**4. `DatagramSocket.connect()` succeeds—does that prove the server has the UDP port open?** No. This is not a TCP handshake.

**5. `PortUnreachableException` does not appear—does that prove the destination is reachable?** No. The Javadoc only promises it **may** be thrown in the corresponding situation, with no guarantee.

**6. Is `SocketTimeoutException` at the receiver an EOF?** No. UDP has no stream EOF; timeout only marks that **the wait has timed out**.

**7. The ACK is lost after the server has executed the business operation. What can happen on retry?** The server may receive the same request again; idempotency/dedup and durable state are needed if an exactly-once effect is required.

**8. The receive buffer is 512 bytes, but the datagram is 900 bytes. Can the remaining 388 bytes be read in a second `receive` call?** No. A datagram longer than the limit may be truncated; the remainder is not preserved as the next part of a stream.

**9. Can packet loss be fixed by increasing the `ServerSocket` `backlog`?** No. `ServerSocket` is a TCP API; UDP uses `DatagramSocket` and is subject to different queues/timeouts/policies.

**Takeaway:** UDP preserves the concept of a **datagram**, but does not keep promises of **complete, unique, and in-order delivery**. A `send()` that throws no error is a **local** observation, while delivery/processing guarantees must come from the **application protocol**: sequence/session IDs, ACKs, timeouts, controlled retransmission, deduplication, reorder buffers, and idempotency. Local error reporting or ICMP only helps with **partial** diagnosis and does not replace end-to-end confirmation.
