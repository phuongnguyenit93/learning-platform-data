# Limits of Protocol Guarantees When a Source Cannot Slow Down

## 1. Problem: The Receiver Is Slower Than the Producer

Suppose a sensor emits **1,000 events/second**, while the application can process only **100 events/second**. The sensor is driven by an external device and keeps sending data even if the application requests less. The gap is then **900 events/second**; if every event is kept in memory, the queue grows continuously. A buffer holding at most 9,000 events will fill up in about **10 seconds** if rates are steady, the queue starts empty, and no other limits exist.

**Reactive Streams** includes a **backpressure** mechanism: the Subscriber tells the Publisher how many items it is ready to receive via **Subscription.request(n)**. However, this signal is **not a physical control command** that forces every sensor, external event source, network protocol, or sending system to slow down.

This is the most important boundary of the topic:

- **The Reactive Streams protocol governs signals transferred from Publisher to Subscriber.**
- **The protocol does not guarantee that the underlying external data source can pause, replay, or emit more slowly.**
- If the source keeps producing data faster than it is consumed, an intermediate component must decide how to handle the excess: **buffer, drop, keep only the latest, offload to durable storage, reject load, or terminate with an error**. Each choice trades off resources, freshness, completeness, and recoverability.

Do not interpret "has backpressure" as "never loses data," "queues can never fill up," or "every external system component automatically slows down."

## 2. What Does Reactive Streams Actually Guarantee?

The Reactive Streams model consists of **Publisher**, **Subscriber**, **Subscription**, and **Processor** (both receiver and producer). The Subscriber receives a Subscription in **onSubscribe**, then signals demand via **request(n)**; the Publisher may emit **onNext(value)** within that demand and then terminate with **onComplete** or **onError**. The Subscriber may also **cancel**.

Key protocol constraints:

| Rule | Practical Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Bounded by demand** | A spec-compliant Publisher **must not emit more onNext signals than the total items requested** on the same Subscription. |
| **request(n) with positive n** | Requests n additional items; requests are cumulative, and do not mean receiving exactly n items immediately. |
| **Unfulfilled/zero demand** | Without demand, the Publisher must not send onNext beyond what was requested; it may wait, hold data in another layer, or handle overload per the source/adapter's policy. |
| **Terminal signal** | A Subscription is terminated by **onComplete** or **onError**, not both; after a terminal signal, no more onNext signals may be sent. Terminal signals do not necessarily require additional requests. |
| **Serial signals** | Signal calls to a Subscriber must be serialized per spec rules, even when the source runs on multiple threads. |
| **Cancellation** | cancel requests stopping the subscription and releasing appropriate resources; due to races across threads, cancellation does not mean every in-flight operation vanishes instantly. |

In this context, **a Publisher "not emitting past demand" is a protocol obligation at the Publisher–Subscriber interface**, not a pledge that every piece of data generated by external systems has a path to the Subscriber.

For instance, suppose the Subscriber only calls request(2). A spec-compliant Publisher may emit at most two onNext signals until further requests arrive. If the adapter has already received five events from the device, the remaining three events **must not be pushed straight through as onNext past demand**. What the adapter does with them depends on the overload policy; Reactive Streams **does not choose that policy for you**.

**request(Long.MAX_VALUE)** is commonly treated as unbounded demand from the Subscriber. By explicitly requesting unbounded, the receiver is no longer using demand to cap item counts; that still does not make the external source automatically safe with respect to memory, processing speed, or fault tolerance.

## 3. Boundary Between a "Push" Source and a Reactive Streams-Compliant Publisher

Picture a data path:

~~~text
Sensor / UDP / third-party callback / external event stream
            (may not support slowing down)
                          |
                          v
               Data-receiving adapter
           +---------------------------+
           | Buffer and overflow policy|
           | Capacity limit            |
           | Drop / latest / error...  |
           +---------------------------+
                          |
                          v
             Demand-compliant Publisher
                          |
                   Subscription
               request(n), cancel()
                          |
                          v
                      Subscriber
~~~

An adapter may receive events through callbacks independent of demand. By design, **callbacks from an external source** and **Subscriber.onNext** are two distinct boundaries:

1. The external source emits whenever it has data.
2. The adapter tracks current demand, maintains queue state, and enforces the overload policy.
3. The adapter invokes onNext downstream only while sufficient demand remains.
4. If excess data cannot be stored or safely discarded, the adapter must signal an error/termination or divert to another storage layer per the business contract.

**The mismatch between production speed and consumption speed is resolved at the adaptation boundary**; it does not magically vanish just by changing types to Flux or Mono.

Some systems do have genuine backpressure mechanisms, such as credit/flow-control protocols, sources supporting pause/resume, consumers actively polling, or queues able to hold backlog. However, you must inspect **each connector/driver and the entire path**. Having backpressure in one layer does not prove the layer before it will stop emitting.

## 4. Why Can't "Keep Everything + Bounded Memory" Be Guaranteed in All Cases?

Notation:

- **R**: number of events generated per second.
- **C**: number of events processed per second.
- **B**: maximum number of events that can wait in the buffer.

When **R > C** continuously and the source receives no slowdown signal, the number of queued items tends to grow by roughly **(R − C) × t** after t seconds, until an intervention policy kicks in. If event rates and sizes vary over time, you must also account for jitter, processing latency, individual item sizes, and auxiliary storage.

With **R = 1,000/sec, C = 100/sec, B = 9,000 items**, the buffer fills in roughly B/(R − C) = **10 seconds**. Doubling B only stretches how long it can hold out; it does not fix the fact that data generation permanently outpaces processing.

If the source cannot slow down, you cannot simultaneously guarantee indefinitely that:

1. **Every event is retained and delivered to the intended receiver**;
2. **Total buffer/storage remains strictly bounded**;
3. **The application never blocks, rejects, or throttles input**;
4. **Input rate continuously exceeds consumption rate**.

To preserve everything over the long term, at least one assumption must change: increase processing capacity, slow down the source, allow input to block or be rejected, or offload data to durable storage with managed capacity and retention policies. Even disk storage or a message broker has limits on capacity, retention, throughput, and failure modes.

This is a **physical/resource constraint**, not an implementation flaw specific to Spring WebFlux, Reactor, or Reactive Streams.

## 5. Handling Policies When the Source Cannot Slow Down

There is no single "best" policy for every data type. You must decide upfront **whether losing an event is acceptable**, **whether older events remain valuable**, **whether replay is possible**, and **how much latency is acceptable**.

| Policy | Behavior When Receiver Is Slow | Trade-offs and Suitable Cases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bounded buffer** | Holds up to B items; must pick a next action when full. | Absorbs short bursts, but does not solve sustained overload; requires monitoring fill level. |
| **Drop newest** | Discards newly arriving items when capacity/demand is exhausted. | Preserves already-queued data; suitable when older data matters more than newer data. |
| **Drop oldest / latest** | Drops older items to favor newer events, or keeps only the latest undelivered value. | Useful for dashboards/current state; loses history and intermediate events. |
| **Sampling / aggregation** | Reduces item count by sampling or aggregating over time windows. | Reasonable for certain metrics/telemetry; does not preserve every raw record. |
| **Error / fail fast** | Emits onError and terminates the stream when limits are exceeded. | Does not silently drop data; downstream needs an explicit retry/reconnect/recovery policy. |
| **Pause, pull, credit** | Slows the upstream layer if it genuinely supports it. | Can preserve more data at this boundary, though backlog may shift upstream. |
| **Durable storage** | Writes events to a log/broker/disk before asynchronous processing. | Improves resilience, but adds latency, cost, operational complexity, and retention/capacity limits. |

**Warning about "unbounded buffers":** An uncapped buffer may mask overload for a while, then drive up heap usage, GC pressure, and eventually trigger OutOfMemoryError. An unbounded buffer is *not* a delivery guarantee.

**Warning about "drop":** The protocol can remain 100% Reactive Streams-compliant while the adapter discards events before emitting them to the Subscriber. Yet **business semantics have changed**: users may see only part of the data. This must be an intentional, metered decision accepted by the business domain.

**Warning about "error":** An onError signal terminates the current Subscription. It does not automatically retry, automatically preserve lost events, or automatically restore upstream. Any retry mechanism needs bounds, backoff, and idempotency checks to avoid duplicate side effects.

## 6. Reactor Example: Source Keeps Pushing, Subscriber Only Requests Two Items

The following example simulates a **push source that ignores demand**: the producer pushes five consecutive numbers into the adapter. The adapter uses **FluxSink.OverflowStrategy.DROP** to deliver only items matching demand. This is a **synchronous simulation** to clarify the Reactive Streams contract; real sensors may emit from other threads, devices, or protocols.

Create a Java 17+ Maven project:

~~~text
reactive-overflow/
├── pom.xml
└── src/main/java/UnstoppableSourceDemo.java
~~~

File **pom.xml**:

~~~xml
<project xmlns="http://maven.apache.org/POM/4.0.0"
         xmlns:xsi="http://www.w3.org/2001/XMLSchema-instance"
         xsi:schemaLocation="http://maven.apache.org/POM/4.0.0 https://maven.apache.org/xsd/maven-4.0.0.xsd">
    <modelVersion>4.0.0</modelVersion>
    <groupId>example</groupId>
    <artifactId>reactive-overflow</artifactId>
    <version>1.0.0</version>

    <properties>
        <maven.compiler.release>17</maven.compiler.release>
        <project.build.sourceEncoding>UTF-8</project.build.sourceEncoding>
    </properties>

    <dependencies>
        <dependency>
            <groupId>io.projectreactor</groupId>
            <artifactId>reactor-core</artifactId>
            <version>3.6.12</version>
        </dependency>
    </dependencies>

    <build>
        <plugins>
            <plugin>
                <groupId>org.codehaus.mojo</groupId>
                <artifactId>exec-maven-plugin</artifactId>
                <version>3.5.0</version>
                <configuration>
                    <mainClass>UnstoppableSourceDemo</mainClass>
                </configuration>
            </plugin>
        </plugins>
    </build>
</project>
~~~

File **src/main/java/UnstoppableSourceDemo.java**:

~~~java
import org.reactivestreams.Subscription;
import reactor.core.publisher.BaseSubscriber;
import reactor.core.publisher.Flux;
import reactor.core.publisher.FluxSink;

public class UnstoppableSourceDemo {
    public static void main(String[] args) {
        Flux<Integer> source = Flux.create(sink -> {
            // Simulate an external source that ignores demand.
            for (int i = 1; i <= 5; i++) {
                sink.next(i);
            }
            sink.complete();
        }, FluxSink.OverflowStrategy.DROP);

        source.subscribe(new BaseSubscriber<Integer>() {
            @Override
            protected void hookOnSubscribe(Subscription subscription) {
                System.out.println("Requested: 2");
                request(2);
            }

            @Override
            protected void hookOnNext(Integer value) {
                System.out.println("Received: " + value);
            }

            @Override
            protected void hookOnComplete() {
                System.out.println("Completed");
            }

            @Override
            protected void hookOnError(Throwable error) {
                System.out.println("Error: " + error.getClass().getSimpleName());
            }
        });
    }
}
~~~

Run with JDK 17+ and Maven:

~~~bash
mvn -q compile exec:java
~~~

Lines printed by the program:

~~~text
Requested: 2
Received: 1
Received: 2
Completed
~~~

**Analysis:**

1. **hookOnSubscribe** sends request(2), so downstream has only two units of demand.
2. The producer loops from 1 to 5 **regardless of demand** and calls sink.next five times.
3. The **DROP** adapter delivers 1 and 2; values 3, 4, and 5 are not converted into onNext because demand is exhausted.
4. The producer calls sink.complete; the Subscriber receives **onComplete**. "Completed" indicates that the Publisher has finished, **not that downstream received all five original events**.

This example intentionally highlights the difference between **the producer generating five events** and **the Subscriber receiving only two events**. The **Publisher → Subscriber** path still honors demand. Events dropped at the adapter are not automatically recovered.

If you replace **DROP** with **ERROR**, emitting past demand can produce an error signal in the adapter per that policy. With **BUFFER**, unrequested items can sit in a queue waiting for future requests; that increases memory consumption and needs a capacity cap. With **LATEST**, undelivered items can be replaced by the most recent item. Do not switch strategies just to "make the error go away"; verify the exact data impact.

## 7. Related Reactor APIs and Their Limits

**Flux.create** and **Flux.push** are commonly used when adapting callback/push APIs into a Flux. Neither turns an external API into a pausable source; the application remains responsible for subscription lifecycle, cancellation, errors, resources, and overflow strategy. **Flux.create** supports callback sources that can emit from multiple threads by design; **Flux.push** is for single-threaded emission sources.

Several useful operators and options:

| Tool | What It Can Do | What It Does Not Guarantee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **onBackpressureBuffer(...)** | Queues when upstream outpaces downstream; has variants with capacity bounds and overflow policies. | Does not create infinite capacity; unbounded variants can exhaust memory. |
| **onBackpressureDrop(...)** | Drops items downstream cannot take in time; can attach a callback to observe dropped items. | Does not preserve complete event delivery. |
| **onBackpressureLatest()** | Retains the latest pending item when downstream is slow. | Does not preserve full order or intermediate history. |
| **onBackpressureError()** | Signals an error when demand cannot keep up at the operator boundary. | Does not save events for recovery after the error. |
| **limitRate(n)** | Shapes how the operator requests batches from a demand-compliant upstream. | Does not command devices or non-backpressured push APIs to slow down. |
| **publishOn(scheduler, prefetch)** | Switches execution context with its own request/buffering mechanism. | Not a free throughput booster; buffer size and latency must be measured. |

**Easy pitfall:** Several operators process events *after they have already entered a Publisher with a specific emission strategy*. If an earlier layer already dropped data silently, an operator placed downstream **cannot detect or recover** events that never reached it. Monitoring must be placed right at the layer receiving the external source.

Additionally, common Reactor sources are often **cold Publishers**: the producer runs only upon subscription and may even obey demand natively, like Flux.range. When testing overflow using Flux.range, the source may pause emission when demand is missing, failing to reproduce the real push-source problem. To test a source that cannot slow down, you must simulate an **arrival rate independent of demand**.

## 8. Real-World Scenarios: When Can Events Be Lost?

### Temperature Dashboards and Instantaneous State

If a UI only needs the **latest** temperature reading, dropping intermediate values between redraws can be reasonable. You can use latest, sampling, or coalesce multiple updates into one state snapshot. However, if the data is used to detect safety threshold breaches or perform historical analysis, you cannot blindly drop extreme readings.

### Audit Logs, Transactions, and Compliance-Critical Events

If **every event matters**, dropping is wrong for the business. You need durable writes, persistence acknowledgments, retries, idempotency, capacity monitoring, and recovery rules. Reactive Streams **does not provide** exactly-once delivery, transactions, retention, or durable delivery on its own. Those guarantees come from storage protocols, brokers, databases, and well-designed processing logic.

### Message Brokers and Queues

Slow consumers can be handled through pause/poll mechanisms, consumer lag, partitions, and retention. Still, backlog may simply move to the **broker**, rather than disappearing. Once retention expires or the broker fills up, you still have to deal with data loss, write rejections, or errors; you cannot infer infinite replay capability just from wrapping with a Flux adapter.

### HTTP Streaming, SSE, and WebSockets

Spring WebFlux can use Flux for streaming responses. **request(n)** inside Reactor is a Reactive Streams signal; an external SSE or WebSocket client does not necessarily have a corresponding request(n) mechanism over the network. TCP has transport-layer flow control, which is **not equivalent** to a policy guaranteeing every business event is stored and replayed. HTTP/TCP buffers, client read speeds, and timeouts still must be managed.

### UDP Sources or Push-Only Devices

UDP has no built-in rate-control feedback or retransmission. If the application reads too slowly, datagrams can drop in the socket/kernel **even before the callback feeding Flux runs**. Subscriber backpressure cannot recover datagrams already dropped at the transport layer. Measure drops across layers and pick an appropriate storage/control protocol if data loss is unacceptable.

## 9. Separating Three Kinds of "Guarantees" in System Design

A solid design must explicitly address at least three layers:

1. **Reactive Streams protocol guarantees:** a spec-compliant Publisher will not invoke onNext on a Subscriber beyond its demand; terminal signals, cancellation, and serialization follow the contract.
2. **Adapter/buffer guarantees:** queue size, when to drop, when to error, whether old or new items take priority, whether durable storage exists, and what cancellation releases.
3. **End-to-end business guarantees:** whether an event is recorded, processed once per business requirements, replayed after failures, ordered, and deduplicated.

These three guarantees **do not imply one another**. A Publisher can be strictly Reactive Streams-compliant while an upstream adapter has already discarded events; a queue may appear empty even though business logs were lost on the network; a broker with persistence still needs duplicate handling on retries.

Questions to answer before picking a policy:

- Does the external source support **pause, pull, retry, or replay**? At which layer?
- What are the average rate, burst peak, event size, and burst duration?
- How much memory/disk is the queue allowed to use at maximum? When full, **which rule is valid for the business**?
- Must **every event, ordering, timestamps, or only the latest state** be preserved?
- If downstream is slow, cancels, or reconnects, how is pending data handled?
- Who acknowledges **receipt** and who acknowledges **successful processing**? Those two events can differ.
- Does monitoring expose **drops before the adapter**, **drops at the adapter**, **drops after the adapter**, and end-to-end latency?

## 10. Testing and Observing Overload

You cannot conclude a system is safe by testing only a few items with an immediately consuming Subscriber. Proactively simulate a **demand-ignoring producer** and a **slow consumer**.

A useful test suite covers:

- **Zero demand:** verify no onNext exceeds demand; observe actual buffering/dropping/error behavior.
- **Finite demand:** request(2), producer generates more than two; verify onNext count, drop traces, and terminal signal per strategy.
- **Bursts and sustained overload:** simulate R > C to check that queues are bounded, how long load is tolerated, and behavior when the cap is hit.
- **Cancel, error, reconnect:** check that callback sources/Subscriptions are unsubscribed and resources freed; verify whether in-flight data is handled per contract.
- **Replay or retry:** check duplicates, out-of-order delivery, and idempotency when using a broker/durable store.

Metrics to measure include **received/accepted/delivered/dropped** rates, current/max queue depth, end-to-end latency, overflow error count, backlog drain time, heap/GC usage, cancellation events, and consumer latency. For WebFlux apps, include event-loop, connection, and network buffer metrics where relevant.

A common implementation bug is **blocking the event loop** with Thread.sleep(), blocking JDBC, or block() during request handling. That reduces consumption rate and worsens overflow. If you must call a blocking API, isolate it properly with a dedicated scheduler/synchronous layer and load-test it; schedulers do not create infinite data guarantees.

## 11. Misconceptions and Fixes

| Misconception | Correct Understanding |
| --- | --- |
| "Once Flux is used, upstream will automatically slow down." | Only an upstream that actually supports throttling responds to demand accordingly. |
| "request(10) promises the receiver will get all 10 values." | It only permits emitting up to 10 additional items; the Publisher may complete early, error, or have no data. |
| "The Subscriber received onComplete, so every original event arrived." | onComplete only closes the stream as seen from the Publisher; an adapter may have dropped events per policy. |
| "A large buffer solves backpressure." | Buffers only absorb temporary mismatches; sustained overload hits the limit. |
| "Backpressure always prevents data loss." | Many policies satisfy demand by dropping or erroring; losslessness is an additional contract. |
| "SSE/TCP/WebSocket automatically carry request(n) all the way to the source." | Reactive Streams backpressure is local between components; transport/application layers have their own mechanisms. |
| "onBackpressureLatest fits every event." | Only suitable when skipping intermediate events is valid; never default to it for audits/transactions. |
| "Retrying after onError always recovers old data." | Retry can resubscribe to the source, yet requires a replay-capable source and deduplication logic to recover properly. |

## 12. Key Conclusion

**Reactive Streams guarantees that onNext emissions from a spec-compliant Publisher will not exceed the Subscriber's demand.** It provides a contract for subscription, requesting, cancellation, and terminal signals. **It does not guarantee that physical sources or external push APIs will slow down, does not guarantee unbounded memory, and does not guarantee lossless delivery by default.**

When a **source cannot slow down**, place an adaptation boundary with bounded resources and an explicit policy: **bounded buffers, drop/latest/sampling if allowed, error/fail-fast, upstream throttling where supported, or durable storage when data must not be lost**. Choose the solution based on the business contract, test with a producer faster than the consumer, and measure where data could be lost *before* it ever reaches the Publisher.
